My favourite books and authors

Douglas Adams

The funniest and cleverest author I have ever read. And it’s not just the Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy trilogy - reading Dirk Gently's Holistic Detective Agency is one of the few times I’ve had to pause reading a book to deal with the sharp, physical pain from laughing so hard. One lesser known work that I loved - Last Chance to See, a short non-fiction work about his adventures finding and documenting endangered species around the world (Komodo dragons and kakapos and shit).

The Beginning of Infinity, David Deutsch

This book has influenced my worldview more than any other in adult life. I already knew to have a growth mindset about myself - but because of Deutsch, I now have a growth mindset about humanity.

And not out of rose-tinted-glass-half-full optimism but because it makes sense given our current best explanations for how reality works. I didn't really understand the multiverse bits of this book, but the epistemology-focused parts (the bulk) are essential reading.

P.G. Wodehouse

The second funniest author I have read after our boy Doug above. But with P.G.-and no one else - I find myself savoring his wit. That shit is truly savory. The Wooster and Jeeves series is my favorite.

Roald Dahl

If you asked me the age of my inner child, I’d say it’s 11. And what was I doing at 11? Tearing through Roald Dahl books. Decades later, my home has close to too many references to his work. The man had a remarkable turn-of-phrase including many made-up words (not to mention names e.g. Augustus Gloop and Bruce Bogtrotter) and his descriptions of food (real and invented). And he also had softness - my favourite Roal Dahl book is Danny the Champion of the World - unlike Dahl's regular cynicalish view of adults, this is a tender story of a parent-child relationship.

Also underrated — Roald Dahl's short stories for adults that are surprisingly (unsurprisingly?) dark and sexual yet still unmistakably him. Confusing in a fun way.

Nassim Taleb

Nassim Taleb — especially in Antifragile — peeled back a couple of layers of reality. Taleb + Deutsch clued me into 1) the limitations of inductive thinking and 2) the importance of error-correction. But unlike Deutsch, Taleb's work has this additional smooth-brained approach to living in a world you — as an individual — cannot fully comprehend.

Note: I studiously avoid his Twitter and you probably should too — the protein is all in the books (though I also regularly return to this commencement address of his from 2016).

In the Land of Invented Languages, Arika Okrent

This book about conlangs helped me begin to understand memes (in the Dawkins sense) years before I read about this formally - regular languages are living, evolving, shape-shifting beasts while invented languages tend to be designed in a vacuum, devoid of life, and therefore doomed to fail.

A Sense of Style, Steven Pinker

The nice thing about books about writing and this one in particular is that you can't write a book about good writing without writing good. Having read Elements of Style in my youth and kind of found it useful, this felt like a step-up because it actually provided explanations for what makes some writing vibrant and some writing flaccid (to use imo his best turn-of-phrase).

Understanding Comics, Scott McCloud

Understanding Comics belongs in the recursive canon - it's a comic book about how to make good comics. And more generally - I learned a few things about good storytelling in general e.g. you always have two goals with telling a story - 1) sharing your message and 2) convincing the audience to keep paying attention.

Arundhati Roy

For me, Arundhati Roy fits in that rare category of writing fiction and non-fiction that I like - I loved The God of Small Things (Booker Prize winning novel) and The Sinner and the Saint (comparing Gandhi and Ambedkar, the title her pov). She's also a magnetic public speaker. Truly a triple-threat.

How to Know a Person, David Brooks

The best book I've read in the last few years. Reading it was a succession of "ah I couldn't put my finger on it" revelations about listening, conversation and connection - all topics with infinite depth.

Brooks inspired a lil eureka moment for me: some people perceive you and it feels like "cold water" while some people see you and it feels like "warm light". Strive for the latter (I have so much more to say about this).

In Other Rooms, Other Wonders, Daniyal Mueenuddin

A collection of short stories mostly set in south Punjab farmland. This book made me realize that desi writing could be terse, charged and reflective - up until then I had almost exclusively read Pakistani and Indian fiction written from the first-person pov of witty, eccentric, flawed self-indulgent male protagonists (e.g. Midnight's Children, Homeboy, and A Case of Exploding Mangoes).

Liu Cixin

The Three-Body Problem trilogy was my first and only exposure to hard science fiction and one of the few mindfucky books I've read - no book or movie prior to that made me feel the arc of humanity across eons. His short stories are also excellent - I especially like Taking Care of God.

Being Mortal, Atul Gawande

I believe everyone should read one non-fiction book contemplating death (and not too many more than that).

This one by Atul Gawande attempts to answer the questions 1) what does it mean to live well till the very end and 2) how can we (especially the medical profession) make that happen? This book especially hit for me because it interwove journalism about the medicalization of death, research about what the dying want, and the aching, beautiful story of his own father's passing.

I have gifted this book often.
